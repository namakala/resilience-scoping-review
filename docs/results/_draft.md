# Thematic Analysis Results

Seven themes were constructed through iterative analysis of the reviewed literature on stress, coping, and resilience. These themes organize into two overarching narratives. The first narrative describes how stress amplifies. The second narrative describes how resilience develops. A final integrative theme synthesizes these findings into a testable simulation framework. Together, these themes reveal that stress and resilience are interrelated processes determined by cognitive appraisal, available resources, and contextual conditions.

## Part I: Stress Amplification

### Theme 1: Appraisal and Resource Mismatch

The literature consistently distinguishes between two types of appraisals. Challenge stressors are associated with positive affect, work engagement, and adaptive outcomes. Hindrance stressors predict emotional exhaustion, anger, and psychological strain (Y. Wang et al., 2016; Kokou-Kpolou et al., 2021). The same objective demand can produce divergent effects depending on how it is cognitively framed.

Beyond the challenge-hindrance taxonomy, three interrelated perceptual dimensions further explain individual differences in stress responses. Predictability refers to the degree to which an individual can anticipate stressors; unpredictable situations heighten stress by reducing perceived preparedness (Y. Wang et al., 2016). Controllability denotes the extent of influence the individual can exert over stressors; a diminished sense of control exacerbates stress perception and lowers stress anticipation (Hou et al., 2019). Cognitive overload compounds these effects (Kokou-Kpolou et al., 2021).

The resource-environment mismatch model explains why identical stressors produce unequal outcomes. Individuals who lack the personal, social, or material resources to meet external demands experience heightened vulnerability regardless of objective stressor severity. This mismatch amplifies outcomes based on coping capacity:

> The positive or negative effect of the stress rests on the nature of the stressor, the person's perception of the stressor and the level of resources the person has at their disposal to deal with the stressor

These appraisal mechanisms account for why some individuals experience growth under pressure while others deteriorate. Furthermore, they identify modifiable factors that can be addressed by interventions, such as appraisal patterns and resource availability.

### Theme 2: Emotional Mediation and Comorbidity

Stress exposure does not produce adverse outcomes through direct causation alone. Anxiety and depression function as transmission mechanisms that link stressful events to downstream psychological and behavioral consequences. Anxiety symptoms fully or partially mediate the relationship between exposure to stressful life events and depressive symptoms. Depression mediates the pathway from stress to alcohol use. Negative affect mediates the link between stress and binge eating. Insomnia and academic burnout serve as additional mediators connecting perceived stress to behavioral outcomes.

> anxiety symptoms fully mediated the relationship between exposure to stress and depression

Somatic complaints emerged as a frequent clinical presentation of this mediated pathway. Medically unexplained physical symptoms represent a common but underrecognized manifestation of stress-related emotional difficulties, particularly in populations where psychological distress carries stigma (Y. Wang & Chen, 2015; Wei et al., 2023). Reward-seeking behaviors such as process smartphone use exemplify how the pursuit of immediate gratification in response to stress can escalate into problematic usage patterns. The self-perpetuating nature of these dynamics is particularly notable: depressive symptoms triggered by stressful life events predict subsequent life events, creating cycles that persist beyond the initial stressor (Okechukwu et al., 2022):

> depressive symptoms triggered by SLEs have a long-lasting impact by in turn predicting later life events, leading to a self-perpetuating cycle of depression and SLEs

At the population level, these mechanisms manifest as widespread prevalence patterns that characterize stress-related conditions as global health concerns. Depression rates among university students range from 12 to 28 percent. Clinically significant anxiety affects approximately one-third of sampled immigrant populations. Psychological distress rose from 18.9 percent pre-pandemic to 27.3 percent during the COVID-19 outbreak. These figures situate stress-related psychological morbidity within the domain of global health challenges.

### Theme 3: Transition and Vulnerability

The analysis revealed that population-level disparities in stress outcomes arise from the convergence of perceived lack of control with contextual vulnerability factors. Perceived lack of control emerged as the foundational cognitive mechanism driving stress pathology (Tsourtos et al., 2019). This pattern appears across organizational constraints, pandemic disruptions, academic examinations, and daily life demands (Li et al., 2022).

University students constitute a higher-risk population than the general public. The transition to independent living, novel academic pressures, and social disruption converge during a critical developmental window, overwhelming coping resources that were adequate in more structured environments:

> university students constitute a higher risk population for depression than the general population

Cultural minority status introduces additional vulnerability layers. Latinx immigrants face both acculturative stress and stigma around psychological distress, which frequently presents as somatic complaints (R. Wang, 2021). Black women experience race-related stress that operates through perseverative cognition and anticipatory mechanisms. Younger adults consistently show the highest perceived adversity and lowest resilience, with the youngest age group identified as the most vulnerable demographic:

> youngest age group (aged 18-34) as the most vulnerable group for mental distress, with the highest perceived adversities and lowest resilience relative to other age groups

Gender differences further differentiate these patterns: females consistently report higher rates of depression and anxiety, while men show higher alcohol misuse and academic burnout. Occupational groups including healthcare workers, midwives, and rural residents face elevated stress due to high clinical workloads, inadequate institutional support, and limited access to resources (Wei et al., 2023). Each of these groups requires differentiated solutions because the mechanisms driving their vulnerability differ. Caregivers, medical staff, and rural residents face different combinations of demands and constraints that cannot be addressed through uniform approaches.

## Part II: Resilience Protection and Development

### Theme 4: Dynamic Resilience Process

Resilience is best understood as a changeable capacity that develops through exposure to and successful negotiation of stressors. Three factors consistently emerged as enabling this adaptive process. Self-regulation determines behavior amidst challenges, enabling individuals to perceive stressful situations more positively and maintain goal-directed functioning. Emotional stability promotes a balanced emotional state, preventing individuals from being overwhelmed by setbacks and facilitating adaptive coping. Cognitive flexibility enables individuals to shift focus from stressors to constructive problem-solving. All three factors can be learned and strengthened over time (Kokou-Kpolou et al., 2021).

The dynamic nature of resilience is supported by evidence that older adults demonstrate greater resilience than younger cohorts, consistent with resilience developing through accumulated life experience (Crane & Searle, 2016). Workplace experiences modify resilience capacity within relatively short timeframes, indicating that targeted interventions can produce meaningful changes even in adult populations. Challenge stressors, when appraised as manageable, enhance resilience through a positive feedback loop described as a virtuous circle of protective effect amplification:

> constant amplification of the protective effect promotes the virtuous circle of resilience

However, several findings complicate this protective picture. Resilience did not moderate the relationship between daily stressors and negative affect, suggesting that its buffering operates primarily at the level of major life events. Its protective effect was observed for anxiety but not consistently for depression or stress levels. Individuals perceived as resilient exhibited higher rates of negative affect when utilizing emotion-focused coping styles. Resilience failed to predict burnout in some analyses, and its moderating role depended on the timing of measurement, with early measurements sometimes failing to moderate relationships that later measurements captured (Havnen et al., 2020):

> resilience did not moderate the relationship between daily stressors and negative affect

These contradictory findings reveal that resilience is a context-dependent capacity with identifiable boundary conditions. Its protective function varies by stressor type, outcome measure, population, and temporal proximity of assessment. This understanding supports a process-oriented conceptualization of resilience. The factors that promote resilience under one set of circumstances may be insufficient under different conditions.

### Theme 5: Problem-Solving, Coping, and Support

Problem-solving approaches, coping mechanisms, and external support systems are concrete strategies through which resilience is enacted. The capacity to solve problems effectively builds psychological resources and fosters a sense of mastery. Individuals who successfully resolved problems in domains such as employment and romantic relationships experienced measurable increases in mastery and self-efficacy, whereas those who did not attempt to solve their problems or experienced failed attempts demonstrated a decline in both:

> People who effectively solve their problems and have affirming experiences in the face of stressors may find an increase in psychological resources as well as acquiring valuable knowledge

Caregivers play a crucial facilitative role in this process. Parents who manage family challenges effectively transmit resilience to their children through modeling adaptive coping. A sense of being able to support family adjustment emerges as a buffer against mental health problems in high-stress households. This relational dimension of coping extends beyond caregiving to broader support structures.

External support systems were identified as operating through four interconnected pillars: social cohesion (shared norms and trust within communities that drive solidarity), social inclusion (equity and integration ensuring connection to broader networks), social empowerment (using relationships to enhance personal and collective strength), and socioeconomic security (safeguarding against adversity through social justice) (Tsourtos et al., 2019). Beyond these structural pillars, personal resources including hardiness, optimism, self-esteem, and family cohesion provide additional protective effects. Cultural activities contribute to resilience among Latinx immigrants, serving as a resource that buffers against both anxiety and somatization.

The coping strategies individuals adopt fall along a spectrum from adaptive to maladaptive. Problem-focused coping empowers individuals to proactively address stressors, enhancing their sense of control and competence and sustaining an optimistic outlook. Conversely, maladaptive strategies, such as problematic online gaming, alcohol consumption, social media overuse, and hostility, represent escape attempts that amplify distress (Canale et al., 2019; Tudehope et al., 2022):

> highly stressed online gamers may use online gaming (problematically) as a way to relieve their preexisting life stress, which might further magnify their stress experience

The relationship between coping and resilience is reciprocal. Resilience makes individuals more resourceful, enhancing their ability to identify and employ effective coping strategies. In turn, successful use of coping mechanisms fosters personal growth, strengthens resilience, and equips individuals to face future adversities. This cycle of adaptive coping and resilience development contributes to long-term mental and emotional stability.

### Theme 6: Building and Measuring Resilience

The reviewed literature provides evidence that resilience can be systematically cultivated through structured intervention. Active learning during resilience training enables individuals to navigate life setbacks more effectively (Y. Wang et al., 2016). Three skill domains were consistently identified as targets for training programs. Structured anticipation helps individuals constructively identify and evaluate potential adverse events, reducing feelings of helplessness and uncertainty. Cognitive flexibility fosters mental agility, enabling individuals to reassess situations and adjust strategies. Bounce-back skills empower individuals to recover quickly from setbacks, strengthening overall adaptive capacity.

Effective training programs draw on several established intervention modalities. Cognitive-behavioral interventions reframe negative thought patterns that exacerbate stress and depression. Relaxation techniques help individuals stabilize their responses to stressors. Mindfulness-based interventions enhance emotional regulation and enable more flexible responses to adversity. Social support programs build resilience through peer support, mentorship, and community engagement. Skill-building workshops equip individuals with problem-solving, time management, and stress management skills. Comprehensive psychosocial programs integrating these elements show particular promise for high-risk groups, including immigrants, older adults, caregivers, and individuals experiencing chronic stress (Kokou-Kpolou et al., 2021):

> resilience can be improved through active learning and purposeful training

Measurement of resilience remains a contested domain with direct implications for how interventions are designed and evaluated. Traditional instruments operationalize resilience as a fixed trait. However, accumulating evidence supports a shift toward outcome-oriented perspectives that define resilience by the presence of positive adaptation despite adversity. The definition of resilience has changed significantly over the past two decades toward this outcome-oriented approach:

> over the past two decades, the definition of resilience has changed significantly from a trait-oriented approach to an outcome-oriented approach

Current measurement approaches focus predominantly on affective and cognitive dimensions. They neglect bio-behavioural manifestations of resilience, such as physiological regulation, sleep quality, and health behaviors. This gap limits the comprehensiveness of resilience assessment and may constrain the evaluation of interventions targeting these domains. The tension between how resilience is measured and how it actually functions remains an unresolved issue. Resilience is sustained through the interaction of personal and social resources, with positive personality dispositions, family support, and community networks collectively enabling adaptation (Tsourtos et al., 2019).

## Part III: Integrative Synthesis

### Theme 7: A Simulation Framework for Psychological Resilience

The six preceding themes cohere into a testable simulation framework for psychological resilience (Figure 3). This framework operationalizes the dual narrative identified across the analysis. The framework consists of three interconnected modules, each grounded in specific themes from the analysis.

The stress perception module draws on Themes 1 and 2. Stress perception is the product of appraisal processes mediated by emotional states of anxiety and depression. These appraisal processes include challenge versus hindrance evaluation, predictability, controllability, and cognitive load. This module governs how simulated agents interpret environmental demands and progress along pathways toward either adaptive coping or distress escalation. The resource-environment mismatch identified in Theme 1 parameterizes each agent's vulnerability threshold.

The resilience activation module draws on Themes 4, 5, and 6. It defines the mechanisms through which resilience is mobilized: self-regulation, emotional stability, and cognitive flexibility as internal capacities (Theme 4); problem-solving strategies, coping approaches, and external support structures as behavioral and social resources (Theme 5); and structured intervention exposure as a modifiable input (Theme 6). This module governs how agents allocate and optimize available resources to stabilize emotional states and manage stress perception.

The integrative simulation module embeds both perception and activation processes within a dynamic system where agents possess demographic characteristics, psychological capital, and access to external resources. Psychological capital interacts with family support, social networks, and intervention exposure to determine resilience trajectories. Psychological capital is shaped primarily by extraversion and neuroticism: extraversion encompasses sociability and assertiveness, whereas neuroticism is characterized by volatility and withdrawal. Contextual vulnerability factors identified in Theme 3 parameterize agent populations to reflect real-world disparities in stress exposure and resource availability.

Together, these three modules translate the qualitative themes of this analysis into a formal structure for computational simulation, enabling researchers to model the dynamic interplay between stress amplification and resilience protection identified across the reviewed literature.

## Synthesis

The seven themes reveal a dual narrative about stress and resilience. Stress amplifies through appraisal, resource mismatch, and emotional mediation. Resilience develops through dynamic and experience-dependent processes.

Effective problem-solving and caregiver support facilitate mastery and interrupt the self-perpetuating cycles that characterize stress pathology. Structured interventions can build adaptive capacity in populations, though their design must contend with unresolved questions about how resilience should be measured. Prevention must target cognitive processes and structural conditions simultaneously. At the individual level, this includes restructuring appraisal patterns and building skills through training. At the population level, policies must address the systemic determinants of stress exposure and resource availability. The simulation framework proposed in Theme 7 translates these insights into a testable model, bridging the qualitative findings with a formal computational structure. These themes were constructed through iterative engagement with the evidence and represent an interpretive synthesis of the reviewed literature.
