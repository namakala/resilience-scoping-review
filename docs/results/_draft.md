# Thematic Analysis Results

Seven topics were constructed through iterative analysis of the reviewed literature on stress, coping, and resilience. These topics organized into two narratives. The first narrative described how stress amplified. The second narrative described how resilience developed. A final integrative topic synthesized these findings into a testable simulation framework. Together, these topics revealed that stress and resilience were interrelated processes determined by cognitive appraisal and contextual conditions.

## Part I: Stress Amplification

### Topic 1: Appraisal and Resource Mismatch

The literature consistently distinguished between two types of appraisals. Challenge stressors were associated with positive affect and adaptive outcomes. Hindrance stressors predicted emotional exhaustion and psychological strain. The same objective demand could produce divergent effects depending on how it was cognitively framed.

Beyond the challenge-hindrance taxonomy, three interrelated perceptual dimensions further explained individual differences in stress responses. Predictability referred to the degree to which an individual could anticipate stressors; unpredictable situations heightened stress by reducing perceived preparedness. Controllability denoted the extent of influence the individual could exert over stressors; a diminished sense of control exacerbated stress perception and lowered stress anticipation. Cognitive overload compounded these effects.

The resource-environment mismatch model explained why identical stressors produced unequal outcomes. Individuals who lacked the personal or social resources to meet external demands experienced heightened vulnerability regardless of objective stressor severity. This mismatch amplified outcomes based on coping capacity:

> The positive or negative effect of the stress rests on the nature of the stressor, the person's perception of the stressor and the level of resources the person has at their disposal to deal with the stressor

These appraisal mechanisms accounted for why some individuals experienced growth under pressure while others deteriorated. They identified modifiable factors that could be addressed by interventions, such as appraisal patterns and resource availability.

### Topic 2: Emotional Mediation and Comorbidity

Stress exposure did not produce adverse outcomes through direct causation alone. Anxiety and depression were transmission mechanisms that linked stressful events to downstream psychological and behavioral consequences. Anxiety symptoms fully or partially mediated the relationship between exposure to stressful life events and depressive symptoms. Depression mediated the pathway from stress to alcohol use. Negative affect mediated the link between stress and binge eating. Insomnia and academic burnout were additional mediators connecting perceived stress to behavioral outcomes.

> anxiety symptoms fully mediated the relationship between exposure to stress and depression

Somatic complaints emerged as a frequent clinical presentation of this mediated pathway. Medically unexplained physical symptoms were a common but underrecognized manifestation of stress-related emotional difficulties, particularly in populations where psychological distress carried stigma. Reward-seeking behaviors such as process smartphone use exemplified how the pursuit of immediate gratification in response to stress could escalate into problematic usage patterns. The self-perpetuating nature of these patterns was notable: depressive symptoms triggered by stressful life events predicted subsequent life events. These cycles persisted beyond the initial stressor:

> depressive symptoms triggered by SLEs have a long-lasting impact by in turn predicting later life events, leading to a self-perpetuating cycle of depression and SLEs

At the population level, these mechanisms manifested as widespread prevalence patterns that characterized stress-related conditions as global health concerns. Depression rates among university students ranged from 12 to 28 percent. Clinically significant anxiety affected approximately one-third of sampled immigrant populations. Psychological distress rose from 18.9 percent pre-pandemic to 27.3 percent during the COVID-19 outbreak. These figures situated stress-related psychological morbidity within the domain of global health challenges.

### Topic 3: Transition and Vulnerability

The analysis revealed that population-level disparities in stress outcomes arose from the convergence of perceived lack of control with contextual vulnerability factors. Perceived lack of control emerged as the foundational cognitive mechanism driving stress pathology. This pattern appeared across organizational constraints, pandemic disruptions, academic examinations, and daily life demands.

University students constituted a higher-risk population than the general public. The transition to independent living and novel academic pressures converged during a critical developmental window. They overwhelmed coping resources that were adequate in more structured environments:

> university students constitute a higher risk population for depression than the general population

Cultural minority status introduced additional vulnerability layers. Latinx immigrants faced both acculturative stress and stigma around psychological distress, which frequently presented as somatic complaints. Black women experienced race-related stress that operated through perseverative cognition and anticipatory mechanisms. Younger adults consistently showed the highest perceived adversity and lowest resilience, with the youngest age group identified as the most vulnerable demographic:

> youngest age group (aged 18-34) as the most vulnerable group for mental distress, with the highest perceived adversities and lowest resilience relative to other age groups

Gender differences shaped these patterns: females consistently reported higher rates of depression and anxiety, while men showed higher alcohol misuse and academic burnout. Occupational groups including healthcare workers and rural residents faced elevated stress due to high clinical workloads and limited access to resources. Each of these groups required differentiated solutions because the mechanisms driving their vulnerability differed. Caregivers and rural residents faced different combinations of demands and constraints that could not be addressed through uniform approaches.

## Part II: Resilience Protection and Development

### Topic 4: Resilience as a Changeable Capacity

Resilience was best understood as a changeable capacity that developed through exposure to and successful negotiation of stressors. Three factors consistently emerged as enabling this adaptive process. Ego-resiliency adapted impulse control to context, allowing flexible responses to demands. Cognitive flexibility selected adaptive information processing modes rather than defaulting to threat bias. Continued functioning under stress carried out daily tasks despite adversity. All three factors could be learned and strengthened over time.

Evidence from age comparisons supported this understanding. Older adults demonstrated greater resilience than younger cohorts, consistent with resilience developing through accumulated life experience. Workplace experiences modified resilience capacity within relatively short timeframes. This suggested that targeted interventions could produce meaningful changes even in adult populations. Challenge stressors, when appraised as manageable, enhanced resilience through a positive feedback loop described as a virtuous circle of protective effect amplification:

> constant amplification of the protective effect promotes the virtuous circle of resilience

Several findings complicated this protective picture. Resilience did not moderate the relationship between daily stressors and negative affect. Its buffering operated primarily at the level of major life events. Its protective effect was observed for anxiety but not consistently for depression or stress levels. Individuals perceived as resilient exhibited higher rates of negative affect when utilizing emotion-focused coping styles. Resilience failed to predict burnout in some analyses, and its moderating role depended on the timing of measurement. Early measurements sometimes failed to moderate relationships that later measurements captured:

> resilience did not moderate the relationship between daily stressors and negative affect

These contradictory findings revealed that resilience was a context-dependent capacity with identifiable boundary conditions. Its protective function varied by stressor type, outcome measure, population, and temporal proximity of assessment. This understanding supported a process-oriented conceptualization of resilience. The factors that promoted resilience under one set of circumstances might be insufficient under different conditions.

### Topic 5: Problem-Solving, Coping, and Support

Problem-solving approaches and coping mechanisms were concrete strategies through which resilience was enacted. The capacity to solve problems effectively built psychological resources and created a sense of mastery. Individuals who successfully resolved problems in domains such as employment and romantic relationships experienced measurable increases in mastery and self-efficacy, whereas those who did not attempt to solve their problems or experienced failed attempts demonstrated a decline in both:

> People who effectively solve their problems and have affirming experiences in the face of stressors may find an increase in psychological resources as well as acquiring valuable knowledge

Caregivers facilitated this process. Parents who managed family challenges effectively transmitted resilience to their children through modeling adaptive coping. A sense of being able to support family adjustment emerged as a buffer against mental health problems in high-stress households. This relational dimension of coping extended beyond caregiving to broader support structures.

Support structures operated through multiple layers. Positive personal dispositions such as hardiness and optimism provided foundational protection. Family cohesion and external social support extended this foundation. Cultural strengths including familism, religiosity, biculturalism, and community support buffered stress and promoted adaptation, particularly among Latinx immigrants and other marginalized populations.

The coping strategies individuals adopted fell along a spectrum from adaptive to maladaptive. Problem-focused coping empowered individuals to proactively address stressors. It enhanced their sense of control and competence and sustained an optimistic outlook. Conversely, maladaptive strategies, such as problematic online gaming, alcohol consumption, social media overuse, and hostility, were escape attempts that amplified distress:

> highly stressed online gamers may use online gaming (problematically) as a way to relieve their preexisting life stress, which might further magnify their stress experience

The relationship between coping and resilience was reciprocal. Resilience made individuals more resourceful and enhanced their ability to identify and employ effective coping strategies. In turn, successful use of coping mechanisms built personal growth and equipped individuals to face future adversities. This cycle of adaptive coping and resilience development contributed to long-term mental and emotional stability.

### Topic 6: Building and Measuring Resilience

The reviewed literature provided evidence that resilience could be systematically cultivated through structured intervention. Active learning during resilience training enabled individuals to handle life setbacks more effectively. Three skill domains were consistently identified as targets for training programs. Structured anticipation helped individuals constructively identify and evaluate potential adverse events and reduced feelings of helplessness and uncertainty. Cognitive flexibility built mental agility and enabled individuals to reassess situations and adjust strategies. Bounce-back skills empowered individuals to recover quickly from setbacks and strengthen overall adaptive capacity.

Effective training programs drew on several established intervention modalities. Cognitive-behavioral interventions reframed negative thought patterns that exacerbated stress and depression. Relaxation techniques helped individuals stabilize their responses to stressors. Mindfulness-based interventions enhanced emotional regulation. Social support programs built resilience through peer support, mentorship, and community engagement. Comprehensive psychosocial programs integrating these elements showed particular promise for high-risk groups, including immigrants, older adults, caregivers, and individuals experiencing chronic stress:

> One promising feature of resilience is that it can be improved through active learning and purposeful training

Measurement of resilience remained a contested domain with direct implications for how interventions were designed and evaluated. Traditional instruments treated resilience as a fixed trait. Accumulating evidence supported a shift toward outcome-oriented perspectives that defined resilience by the presence of positive adaptation despite adversity. The definition of resilience had changed significantly over the past two decades toward this outcome-oriented approach:

> over the past two decades, the definition of resilience has changed significantly from a trait-oriented approach to an outcome-oriented approach

Measurement approaches in the reviewed studies focused predominantly on affective and cognitive dimensions. They neglected bio-behavioural manifestations of resilience, such as physiological regulation, sleep quality, and health behaviors. This gap limited the comprehensiveness of resilience assessment and might constrain the evaluation of interventions targeting these domains. The tension between how resilience was measured and how it actually functioned remained an unresolved issue. Resilience was sustained through the interaction of personal and social resources, with positive personality dispositions and community networks enabling adaptation.

## Part III: Integrative Synthesis

### Topic 7: Simulation Framework for Psychological Resilience

The six preceding topics cohered into an integrated understanding of stress and resilience. Perceived lack of control and resource-environment mismatch served as cognitive precursors driving stress amplification, while emotional mediation pathways acted as the mechanistic bridge linking appraisal processes to psychological consequences. Problem complexity emerged as greatest when these cognitive dynamics coincided with contextual vulnerability factors such as developmental stage, occupational role, and cultural background. Resilience operated as both developmental outcome and protective process, with structured interventions capable of building adaptive capacity through skills training, caregiver facilitation, and resource mobilization. However, the protective effects of resilience were context-dependent, varying by stressor type, outcome measure, population, and temporal proximity of assessment. These findings implied that effective prevention must target both personal coping resources and systemic conditions that create and sustain heightened exposure to adversity.

**Author's proposed extension.** Building on this grounded foundation, a simulation framework can translate these qualitative insights into a formal computational structure for modeling the interplay between stress amplification and resilience protection.

The stress perception module draws on Topics 1 and 2. Stress perception is the product of appraisal processes mediated by emotional states of anxiety and depression. These appraisal processes include challenge versus hindrance evaluation, predictability, controllability, and cognitive load. This module governs how simulated agents interpret environmental demands and progress along pathways toward either adaptive coping or distress escalation. The resource-environment mismatch identified in Topic 1 parameterizes each agent's vulnerability threshold.

The resilience activation module draws on Topics 4, 5, and 6. It defines the mechanisms through which resilience is mobilized: ego-resiliency, cognitive flexibility, and continued functioning under stress as internal capacities (Topic 4); problem-solving strategies, coping approaches, and external support structures as behavioral and social resources (Topic 5); and structured intervention exposure as a modifiable input (Topic 6). This module governs how agents allocate and optimize available resources to stabilize emotional states and manage stress perception.

The integrative module embeds both perception and activation processes within a changing system where agents possess demographic characteristics and access to external resources. Psychological capital interacts with family support, social networks, and intervention exposure to determine resilience trajectories. This framework serves as a testable model for researchers to explore stress-resilience dynamics across diverse populations and contexts.

## Synthesis

The seven topics revealed a dual narrative about stress and resilience. Stress amplified through appraisal, resource mismatch, and emotional mediation. Resilience developed through experience-dependent processes.

Effective problem-solving and caregiver support facilitated mastery and interrupted the self-perpetuating cycles that characterized stress pathology. Structured interventions could build adaptive capacity in populations, though their design had to contend with unresolved questions about how resilience should be measured. Prevention had to target cognitive processes and structural conditions simultaneously. At the individual level, this included restructuring appraisal patterns and building skills through training. At the population level, policies had to address the systemic determinants of stress exposure and resource availability. The simulation framework proposed in Topic 7 translated these insights into a testable model. It bridged the qualitative findings with a formal computational structure. These topics were constructed through iterative engagement with the evidence and were an interpretive synthesis of the reviewed literature.
