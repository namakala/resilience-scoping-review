# Transition-Related Emotional Strain and Contextual Vulnerability Factors in Problem Complexity and Severity

The experience of perceived lack of control over life events initiates a cascade of psychological consequences that are amplified by emotional and psychological mediation pathways. These pathways shape how individuals appraise stressors and experience the gap between personal resources and external demands, ultimately determining the scope and severity of the resulting problem. Contextual vulnerability factors including developmental stage, occupational role, cultural background, and socioeconomic conditions further compound this dynamic by increasing both the likelihood of stressor exposure and the severity of its impact. Student populations and marginalized groups are particularly affected, as they navigate transitions and adverse conditions that simultaneously increase demand and deplete available resources.

## Perceived Lack of Control

Perceived lack of control emerged as the foundational cognitive mechanism driving stress-related pathology, operationalized as the appraisal that environmental difficulties exceed an individual's capacity to manage them.[^1] This pattern was observed across diverse stressors ranging from organizational constraints and pandemic-related disruptions to academic examinations and daily life demands.[^2] Stressful life circumstances including acculturative stress, minority status, perseverative cognition, and the loss of a spouse functioned as potent triggers of this mechanism.[^3] Academic and financial pressures among university students, alongside the challenges of early career transitions in demanding professions, further compounded the sense of diminished control.[^4] The COVID-19 pandemic provided a naturalistic demonstration of this process at scale: quarantine, infection, bereavement, financial difficulties, food insecurity, social isolation, and domestic disruption generated widespread perceptions of uncontrollability that translated into elevated rates of anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress.[^5] Healthcare workers, in particular, endured the compounding effects of overload, inadequate protection, heightened contagion risk, and moral distress, resulting in sustained psychological burden that persisted beyond the acute phase of the pandemic.[^6]

## Contextual Vulnerability Factors

Specific life stages, demographic characteristics, and occupational contexts systematically increased vulnerability to stress-related problems.[^7] University students constituted a higher-risk population than the general public, with the transition to independent living, academic pressures, and social disruption converging during a critical developmental window.[^8] Cultural minority status introduced additional vulnerability layers: Latinx immigrants faced both acculturative stress and stigma around psychological distress, while Black women experienced race-related stress that operated through perseverative cognition and anticipatory mechanisms.[^9] Younger adults and students in the earliest years of their programmes showed the highest perceived adversity and lowest resilience, whereas older adults demonstrated greater adaptive capacity.[^10] Gender differences were observed across multiple studies: females consistently reported greater depression and anxiety, yet men were particularly vulnerable to extended periods of increased depressive symptoms and showed higher rates of alcohol misuse and academic burnout.[^11] Occupational groups including healthcare workers, midwives, civil servants, and international construction personnel faced elevated stress due to high clinical workload, inadequate institutional support, and the inherent high-risk nature of their work.[^12] Hostile coping styles and behavioural dispositions further amplified vulnerability, indicating that the interaction between individual characteristics and environmental demands produced the most severe outcomes when both were unfavourable.[^13]

## Cross-Theme Synthesis

The two themes together reveal that problem complexity is greatest when perceived lack of control coincides with contextual vulnerability. Students, minorities, and frontline workers who already face elevated demands are simultaneously more likely to encounter uncontrollable stressors and less likely to possess the resources needed to manage them. The interaction between individual cognitive appraisal and structural vulnerability suggests that effective prevention must target both levels: building personal coping resources while also addressing the systemic conditions that create and sustain heightened exposure to adversity.
